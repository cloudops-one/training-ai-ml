## 1. The problem

Two complaints about the K-Means personas reached you this week.

From finance: *"Your Champions group includes a few accounts that look nothing like normal customers: hundreds of orders, six-figure spend. K-Means forced them into a group anyway. Can the method just say 'these don't fit'?"*

From marketing: *"Some customers sit right between Regulars and Champions. Labelling them one or the other feels arbitrary. Can we get a 'how sure are you' number?"*

K-Means cannot do either. It puts **every** customer in **exactly one** cluster, and its clusters are always round blobs around a centre. This lesson adds two methods that relax those rules:

- **DBSCAN** finds clusters of any shape and labels points that belong nowhere as **noise**.
- **Gaussian mixture models** give every customer a **probability** of belonging to each cluster.

## 2. The idea

**DBSCAN: crowds in a park.** Look down at a park from above. A *crowd* is a place where many people stand close together. Someone at the edge of a crowd belongs to it, even if they themselves are not surrounded. Someone sitting alone on a bench far from everyone belongs to no crowd. Crowds can be any shape: a queue snaking around an ice-cream van is still one crowd. You never said how many crowds to look for; you only said what "crowded" means.

**Gaussian mixture: overlapping hills.** Imagine each group of customers as a hill of sand: tall in the middle, sloping off at the edges, and some hills wider than others. Where two hills overlap, a grain of sand could have come from either. A mixture model estimates the shape of every hill and then says, for each grain, *"70% likely from hill A, 30% from hill B"*.

## 3. Definition

**DBSCAN** (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) has two settings:

- **eps** ($\varepsilon$): the radius of a point's neighbourhood.
- **min_samples**: how many points (counting itself) must be within eps for the point to be "in a crowd".

Every point is one of three kinds:

- **Core point**: has at least `min_samples` points within eps (itself included).
- **Border point**: not core, but within eps of a core point.
- **Noise point**: neither. Label −1.

A cluster is a set of core points that can reach each other through chains of neighbouring core points, plus the border points attached to them. **You do not choose the number of clusters**; it falls out of eps and min_samples.

**Gaussian mixture model (GMM)**: assumes the data was generated by $k$ overlapping **Gaussian** (bell-shaped, normal) distributions, each with its own centre, spread and shape, and a **weight** $\pi_j$ (its share of the data). Fitting it estimates those; `predict_proba` then gives each point's probability of belonging to each component. This is **soft clustering**, as opposed to K-Means' **hard** assignments.

## 4. Formula

**DBSCAN** uses only distance:

$$N_\varepsilon(p) = \{\, q : d(p, q) \le \varepsilon \,\} \qquad p \text{ is core if } |N_\varepsilon(p)| \ge \text{min\_samples}$$

**GMM soft membership.** For one feature, with component $j$ having weight $\pi_j$, mean $\mu_j$ and standard deviation $\sigma_j$:

$$P(j \mid x) = \frac{\pi_j \, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_j, \sigma_j)}{\sum_{l} \pi_l \, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_l, \sigma_l)} \qquad \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

How likely $x$ is under each bell curve, weighted by how common that group is, rescaled so the probabilities add to 1. (The bell-curve formula is the normal distribution; you do not need to memorise it, only to see that it is largest at $\mu$ and falls off faster when $\sigma$ is small.)

## 5. By hand

### DBSCAN on ten points

eps = 1.5, min_samples = 3.

| point | P | Q | R | S | T | U | V | W | X | Y |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $x$ | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 6 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 4 |
| $y$ | 1 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 6 | 7 | 6 | 7 | 8 |

Count each point's neighbours within 1.5 (itself included). Distances of 1 (side by side) and $\sqrt{2} = 1.41$ (diagonal) count; 2 or more does not.

| point | neighbours within 1.5 | count | kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| P (1, 1) | P, Q, R, S | 4 | core |
| Q (1, 2) | Q, P, R, S | 4 | core |
| R (2, 1) | R, P, Q, S, T | 5 | core |
| S (2, 2) | S, P, Q, R, T | 5 | core |
| T (3, 2) | T, R, S | 3 | core |
| U (6, 6) | U, V, W | 3 | core |
| V (6, 7) | V, U, W | 3 | core |
| W (7, 6) | W, U, V, X | 4 | core |
| X (8, 7) | X, W | 2 | **border** (next to core W) |
| Y (4, 8) | Y | 1 | **noise** |

Core points P, Q, R, S, T all reach each other: **cluster 1**. Core points U, V, W reach each other, and X hangs on to W: **cluster 2**. Y is alone: **noise**. Two clusters were found without anyone asking for two.

### GMM on one feature

Two groups of customers by average order value: group 1 has mean £20, standard deviation £5; group 2 has mean £50, standard deviation £10; each is half the customers ($\pi_1 = \pi_2 = 0.5$). A customer has average order **£32**. Which group?

- Group 1: $\mathcal{N}(32 \mid 20, 5) = \frac{1}{5\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{12^2}{2 \cdot 25}} = \frac{e^{-2.88}}{12.53} = 0.00448$
- Group 2: $\mathcal{N}(32 \mid 50, 10) = \frac{1}{10\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{18^2}{2 \cdot 100}} = \frac{e^{-1.62}}{25.07} = 0.00790$

$$P(\text{group 1} \mid 32) = \frac{0.5 \times 0.00448}{0.5 \times 0.00448 + 0.5 \times 0.00790} = 0.36 \qquad P(\text{group 2} \mid 32) = 0.64$$

£32 is *closer* to group 1's centre (12 away vs 18), so K-Means would put this customer in group 1. The mixture says group 2 is more likely, because group 2 is **wider**: £32 is only 1.8 of group 2's standard deviations from its centre, but 2.4 of group 1's. Mixtures understand that clusters have different spreads.

## 6. In code

```python
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN
labels = DBSCAN(eps=1.5, min_samples=3).fit_predict(points)   # -1 = noise

from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=4, random_state=42).fit(X)
gm.predict(X)          # hard labels, like K-Means
gm.predict_proba(X)    # one probability per component, rows sum to 1
```

**Choosing eps.** For each point, compute the distance to its `min_samples`-th nearest neighbour, sort these distances and plot them. In crowded areas they are small; at the edges of crowds they shoot up. Choose eps near the bend.

## 7. On real data

**Shapes K-Means cannot handle.** On the classic two-moons dataset (two interlocking crescents, 300 points), K-Means splits the picture with a straight line and agrees with the true crescents only slightly (adjusted Rand index **0.25**). DBSCAN with eps = 0.2 recovers both crescents exactly (**1.00**).

**ShopWave customers** (RFM logged and standardised, min_samples = 6). The k-distance plot bends between about 0.3 and 0.6 (the 90th percentile of 6th-neighbour distances is 0.31, the 98th is 0.57):

| eps | clusters | noise customers |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 28 | 797 |
| 0.3 | 5 | 305 |
| 0.4 | 5 | 139 |
| 0.5 | 5 | 73 |

With eps = 0.4, DBSCAN does something K-Means never could: it labels **139 customers as noise**, and they are exactly finance's odd accounts: median spend £7,205, more than ten times the typical customer. They are previewed again in 06.8 as anomalies.

The clusters themselves are less useful for marketing. Frequency is a whole number, so customers with exactly 1 order form a dense sheet, customers with 2 orders another, and so on; DBSCAN follows those sheets (one-order customers, two-order customers, regulars) rather than giving balanced personas. **DBSCAN is excellent at finding noise and odd shapes, and not designed to slice one continuous cloud into marketing segments.**

**Gaussian mixture, 4 components**: sizes 1,493 / 1,098 / 912 / 835. Look closely at the two biggest and smallest: 1,493 is *exactly* the customers with one order and 835 *exactly* those with two. Like DBSCAN, the mixture found the whole-number sheets, and it spent two of its four bell curves on them (each flattened to zero thickness in the frequency direction). The other two components split everyone with 3 or more orders into "moderate" (3–7 orders) and "heavy" (3–209 orders, recent, high spend).

Most customers are confidently placed (79% have a top probability above 0.9), but 3% have no component above 0.6. Those are marketing's "in-between" customers, and now you can list them: they all have a few orders and sit on the border between the moderate and heavy components. The mixture's groups agree with K-Means' only moderately (adjusted Rand index 0.43).

## 8. Where it breaks

**DBSCAN**

- **One eps for the whole dataset.** If some clusters are dense and others sparse, no single radius suits both. (HDBSCAN, also in scikit-learn, handles varying density.)
- **Sensitive to eps.** 0.2 → 28 clusters and 797 noise points; 0.5 → 5 clusters and 73 noise points. Always look at the k-distance plot and try a few values.
- **Struggles in many dimensions**, where all distances become similar. Reduce dimensions first (06.6).
- **Scale matters**, as for every distance method.

**Gaussian mixtures**

- **Assumes bell-shaped clusters.** Skewed or discrete features (like order counts) bend the fit: on ShopWave it spent two of four components on the "exactly 1 order" and "exactly 2 orders" sheets.
- **You still choose $k$.** BIC/AIC scores can guide you, but they too are fooled by discrete features.
- **Can be unstable**: use `n_init` > 1 and check results across seeds.

## 10. Recap

- **DBSCAN** grows clusters from crowded **core** points, attaches **border** points and labels the rest **noise**. No $k$ needed; set eps (with a k-distance plot) and min_samples.
- On ten points it found 2 clusters and 1 noise point; on two moons it scored ARI 1.00 vs K-Means' 0.25; on ShopWave it flagged 139 unusual high-value customers as noise.
- **Gaussian mixtures** model clusters as overlapping bell curves of different widths and give **soft** memberships: £32 was 36% group 1, 64% group 2, despite being nearer group 1's centre. On ShopWave they flagged 3% of customers as genuinely in-between, but also bent around the whole-number order counts.
- Use DBSCAN for odd shapes and noise, GMM when "how sure?" matters, K-Means for quick, round, balanced segments.
